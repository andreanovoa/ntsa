Minimum embedding dimension: Cao's method, false nearest neighbours and Takens' bound¶

A delay embedding reconstructs an attractor from one scalar record $x(t)$ by the delay vectors

$$ \boldsymbol{y}_i(d) = \left( x_i,\ x_{i+\zeta},\ \dots,\ x_{i+(d-1)\zeta} \right) \in \mathbb{R}^d , $$

where $\zeta$ is the delay (in samples) and $d$ the embedding dimension. If $d$ is too small, the reconstruction folds the attractor onto itself; if $d$ is too large, the reconstruction wastes data and amplifies noise. This notebook compares three ways of choosing $d$, all in ntsa.tools:

Function Method What it returns
cao_embedding_dimension(x, lag, ...) Cao (1997): saturation of $E_1(d)$ estimated minimum dimension, $E_1(d)$, $E_2(d)$
false_nearest_neighbours(x, lag, ...) Kennel, Brown & Abarbanel (1992) estimated minimum dimension, false-neighbour fractions
takens_bound(D) Takens (1981); Sauer, Yorke & Casdagli (1991) upper bound $\lfloor 2D + 1 \rfloor$, the dimension $D$

The first two estimate the minimum embedding dimension $m_\mathrm{min}$ from the data. The third bounds it from the dimension $D$ of the system: Takens' theorem guarantees an embedding for every $m \ge 2D + 1$, so

$$ D < m_\mathrm{min} \le \lfloor 2D + 1 \rfloor , $$

where the lower bound holds because the reconstruction must contain the attractor. The bound is a sufficient condition, so the data-driven estimates typically lie below it. We test the three on Lorenz-63, one site of Lorenz-96 and white noise, and then analyse how the estimates depend on the length of the series and on observation noise.

1. Cao's method¶

For each delay vector $\boldsymbol{y}_i(d)$, let $\boldsymbol{y}_{n(i,d)}(d)$ be its nearest neighbour in the maximum norm, excluding temporal neighbours $\lvert i - n \rvert \le w$ (the Theiler window $w$). Cao (1997) measures how much the neighbour distance grows when the $(d+1)$-th coordinate is added,

$$ a(i, d) = \frac{\lVert \boldsymbol{y}_i(d+1) - \boldsymbol{y}_{n(i,d)}(d+1) \rVert_\infty} {\lVert \boldsymbol{y}_i(d) - \boldsymbol{y}_{n(i,d)}(d) \rVert_\infty}, \qquad E(d) = \langle a(i, d) \rangle_i, \qquad E_1(d) = \frac{E(d+1)}{E(d)} , $$

where $\langle \cdot \rangle_i$ is the average over the reference points. False neighbours make $a(i,d)$ large; once $d$ unfolds the attractor, adding a coordinate no longer changes the neighbourhoods and $E_1(d)$ stops changing. cao_embedding_dimension returns the first $d$ at which successive values of $E_1$ differ by less than a tolerance tol. Unlike false nearest neighbours, the method has no distance threshold to choose.

Because $E_1$ also saturates for random data, Cao defines a second quantity from the added coordinate alone,

$$ E^*(d) = \langle \lvert x_{i+d\zeta} - x_{n(i,d)+d\zeta} \rvert \rangle_i, \qquad E_2(d) = \frac{E^*(d+1)}{E^*(d)} . $$

For random data the future value does not depend on the past, so $E_2(d) \approx 1$ for every $d$. For deterministic data $E_2(d)$ departs from 1 at small $d$, where close neighbours have close futures.

3. Estimates and bounds¶

For each case we compute $E_1$, $E_2$ and the false-neighbour fraction for $d = 1, \dots, 12$. For Takens' bound we need $D$, which we take in two ways. The first is the Kaplan-Yorke dimension $D_\mathrm{KY}$ of the Lyapunov spectrum (lyapunov_spectrum, kaplan_yorke), which estimates the attractor dimension from the equations and corresponds to the box-counting reading of Sauer, Yorke & Casdagli (1991). The second is the correlation dimension $D_2$ of the delay embedding (correlation_dimension), which takens_bound computes when it receives the series instead of $D$; this is the only option for measured data. We also report Takens' original reading, in which $D$ is the dimension of the state space that contains the dynamics. White noise has no finite-dimensional attractor, so only the estimate from the series applies.

The correlation dimension of the delay embedding at increasing $d$ is a fourth, classical diagnostic: for a deterministic attractor $D_2$ saturates once $d$ unfolds it, whereas for noise it keeps growing with $d$.

The verdicts answer three questions: whether $E_2$ reads the series as stochastic, whether the two data-driven estimates agree, and whether they lie in $(D, \lfloor 2D+1 \rfloor]$. For a low-dimensional attractor such as Lorenz-63, both estimates are expected inside the interval and below the bound from the state dimension: the bound guarantees an embedding but does not find the smallest one. For a high-dimensional attractor, the number of points needed to populate the neighbourhoods grows exponentially with the dimension, so a finite record can give estimates below $D_\mathrm{KY}$ (Kantz & Schreiber 2004, ch. 9); the lower end of the interval exposes this. The correlation dimension of the embedding is low for the same reason, and so is the bound that takens_bound computes from the series.

$E_2$ also loses contrast on high-dimensional chaos (Cao 1997). When the delay is long compared with the predictability time, the added coordinates of close neighbours decorrelate and $E_2$ approaches 1, although the series is deterministic. The next cell repeats $E_2$ for Lorenz-96 at a quarter of the delay.

4. Dependence on the length of the series¶

We repeat the Lorenz-63 estimates on the first $N$ samples of the record, at fixed delay, and print the shortest record from which each estimate no longer changes. Lorenz-63 is low-dimensional, so a few Lyapunov times of data are expected to suffice; the Lorenz-96 result of section 3 shows that this does not carry over to high-dimensional attractors.

5. Dependence on observation noise¶

We add Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\eta\,\sigma_x$ to the Lorenz-63 record, in which $\sigma_x$ is the standard deviation of the signal and $\eta$ the relative noise level. At the scale of the noise the data fill every dimension, so the neighbour statistics drift towards those of a stochastic series.

Lorenz-63 with observation noise. Left and centre: $E_1(d)$ and $E_2(d)$ at increasing relative noise level $\eta$. Right: the dimension estimated by Cao's method with two saturation tolerances and by false nearest neighbours, with Takens' bound from $D_\mathrm{KY}$ (dotted).

Noise slows the approach of $E_1$ to its plateau, so the saturation test on $E_1$ reports a larger dimension, and it pulls $E_2$ towards 1, the signature of a stochastic series. False nearest neighbours degrades too, because the noise displaces neighbours along the added coordinate and the distance threshold $R_\mathrm{tol}$ then flags them as false. The printed verdict compares how soon each estimate leaves its noiseless value. A larger tolerance in the saturation test makes Cao's estimate less sensitive to noise, at the price of stopping earlier on curves that are still rising; denoising before the embedding is the alternative.

6. Summary¶

  • Cao's $E_1$ and false nearest neighbours estimate the minimum embedding dimension from the data; Cao's method needs no distance threshold, and its $E_2$ separates deterministic from stochastic series, which false nearest neighbours cannot do.
  • Takens' bound $\lfloor 2D + 1 \rfloor$ is a sufficient condition from the dimension of the system. It bounds the minimum embedding dimension from above, so it is a consistency check on the estimates rather than an estimate.
  • On a high-dimensional attractor, a finite record can make both nearest-neighbour methods underestimate the dimension, and a long delay can make $E_2$ read deterministic chaos as noise; the check $D < m_\mathrm{min}$ and a shorter delay expose both.

References. Cao (1997), Physica D 110, 43. Kennel, Brown & Abarbanel (1992), Phys. Rev. A 45, 3403. Takens (1981), Lecture Notes in Math. 898, 366. Sauer, Yorke & Casdagli (1991), J. Stat. Phys. 65, 579. Kantz & Schreiber (2004), Nonlinear Time Series Analysis, CUP.
